# Research Evidence and Peer-Reviewed Papers

This file lists the peer-reviewed papers that have been used to justify or shape major ChoiceTrain features.

These papers inform design choices, classroom workflow decisions, CPAT-style task design, and future EEG/research planning. They do **not** mean that ChoiceTrain is a diagnostic, medical, or clinically validated tool.

## Related Internal Project Document

- `ChoiceTrain.docx`
  This is a client-provided internal project paper draft kept in the wider project papers folder outside this repository. It helped align terminology and the overall system narrative, but it is **not** peer reviewed and should not be treated as evidence in the same way as the papers below.

## 1. CPAT Structure and Attention Training

- Shalev, L., Tsal, Y., & Mevorach, C. (2007). *Computerized progressive attentional training (CPAT) program: Effective direct intervention for children with ADHD*. Child Neuropsychology, 13, 382-388.
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1080/09297040600770787
  Why it matters: this is the core CPAT paper. It directly informed ChoiceTrain's four attention families: sustained, selective-spatial, orienting, and executive attention.

- Muller Spaniol, M., Mevorach, C., Shalev, L., Teixeira, M. C. T. V., Lowenthal, R., & de Paula, C. S. (2021). *Attention training in children with autism spectrum disorder improves academic performance: A double-blind pilot application of the computerized progressive attentional training program*.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34227246/
  Why it matters: this paper helped justify using CPAT-style attention work in school-aged autistic learners and reinforced the importance of measuring academic transfer rather than assuming it.

- Kirk, H., Gray, K., Ellis, K., Taffe, J., & Cornish, K. (2017). *Impact of Attention Training on Academic Achievement, Executive Functioning, and Behavior: A Randomized Controlled Trial*. American Journal on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities, 122(2), 97-117.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28257246/
  Why it matters: this informed the decision to keep attention training connected to school outcomes, behaviour, and executive-function measures for SEND learners.

- Kirk, H. E., Raber, A., Richmond, S., & Cornish, K. M. (2021). *Examining potential predictors of attention training outcomes in children with intellectual and developmental disorders*. Journal of Intellectual & Developmental Disability, 46(3), 197-203.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39818559/
  Why it matters: this supported the learner-profile idea that baseline attention, adaptive functioning, and individual differences may affect training response.

- Powell, G., Wass, S. V., Erichsen, J. T., & Leekam, S. R. (2016). *First evidence of the feasibility of gaze-contingent attention training for school children with autism*. Autism, 20(8), 927-937.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26862085/
  Why it matters: this was used as a cautionary paper around transfer. It supports attention training as promising, but also shows that non-trained gains should be measured directly rather than assumed.

## 2. Teacher Fidelity, Prompting, and Classroom Delivery

- Brady, L., Padden, C., & McGill, P. (2019). *Improving procedural fidelity of behavioural interventions for people with intellectual and developmental disabilities: A systematic review*. Journal of Applied Research in Intellectual Disabilities, 32(4), 762-778.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30968529/
  Why it matters: this directly informed ChoiceTrain's fidelity checklist, support logging, and the decision to make teacher workflow clearer and more structured.

- Yadav, S., Chakraborty, P., Kaul, A., Pooja, & Gupta, B. (2020). *Ability of children to perform touchscreen gestures and follow prompting techniques when using mobile apps*.
  Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7303424/
  Why it matters: this informed touch-first task design, prompt simplification, and the preference for visual or animated prompting over more complex instruction formats for young learners.

- Faja, S., Clarkson, T., Gilbert, R., Vaidyanathan, A., Greco, G., Rueda, M. R., Combita, L. M., & Driscoll, K. (2022). *A preliminary randomized, controlled trial of executive function training for children with autism spectrum disorder*.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34474598/
  Why it matters: this helped justify structured, visually clear executive-function tasks and the use of explicit rule supports for autistic learners.

## 3. Personalisation, Choice, Communication, and Support

- DeLeon, I. G., Fisher, W. W., Rodriguez-Catter, V., Maglieri, K., Herman, K., & Marhefka, J. M. (2001). *Examination of relative reinforcement effects of stimuli identified through pretreatment and daily brief preference assessments*. Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis, 34(4), 463-473.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11800185/
  Why it matters: this paper supported the idea that preferences change over time, which is why ChoiceTrain includes personalisation, preferred content, and the possibility of short daily preference-led adjustments.

- O'Brien, K., and colleagues. (2024). *Mand Modality Preference Assessments among High- and Low-Tech Options for Individuals with Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities: A Systematic Review*.
  Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10891038/
  Why it matters: this informed the communication-support and AAC-aware thinking behind learner personalisation and future modality options.

- Wehmeyer, M. L. (2020). *Self-determination in adolescents and adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities*. Current Opinion in Psychiatry, 33(2), 81-85.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31833947/
  Why it matters: this supported adding learner choice, agency, and support for self-determination rather than making the tool purely adult-directed.

- Lindsay, S., & Varahra, A. (2022). *A systematic review of self-determination interventions for children and youth with disabilities*.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34056997/
  Why it matters: this reinforced the value of learner choice, scaffolding, and multi-component supports in educational settings.

## 4. Gamification and Touch-First Design

- Lumsden, J., Edwards, E. A., Lawrence, N. S., Coyle, D., & Munafo, M. R. (2016). *Gamification of Cognitive Assessment and Cognitive Training: A Systematic Review of Applications and Efficacy*. JMIR Serious Games, 4(2), e11.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27421244/
  Why it matters: this paper shaped the current strategy of using light game framing to improve motivation without letting game elements overwhelm the cognitive task.

- Prins, P. J. M., Dovis, S., Ponsioen, A., ten Brink, E., & van der Oord, S. (2011). *Does computerized working memory training with game elements enhance motivation and training efficacy in children with ADHD?*
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20649448/
  Why it matters: this helped justify adding engaging variants and reward structures cautiously, while still protecting the core attentional rule and measurement integrity.

- Russo-Johnson, C., Troseth, G. L., Duncan, C., & Mesghina, A. (2017). *All Tapped Out: Touchscreen Interactivity and Young Children's Word Learning*.
  Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5388766/
  Why it matters: this informed the choice to keep learner interactions simple, relevant, and on-task rather than filling the interface with decorative or off-topic interactivity.

## 5. EEG / ERP and Research Support

- Brooker, R. J., Bates, J. E., Buss, K. A., Canen, M. J., Dennis-Tiwary, T. A., Gatzke-Kopp, L. M., Hoyniak, C., Klein, D. N., Kujawa, A., Lahat, A., et al. (2020). *Conducting Event-Related Potential (ERP) Research With Young Children: A Review of Components, Special Considerations, and Recommendations for Research on Cognition and Emotion*.
  Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8136588/
  Why it matters: this directly informed ChoiceTrain's event-marker planning, shorter blocks, practice structure, and the need for child-friendly pacing during EEG-linked work.

- Xu, K., and colleagues. (2022). *EEG Data Quality in Real-World Settings: Examining Neural Correlates of Attention in School-Aged Children*. Mind, Brain, and Education.
  Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10760994/
  Why it matters: this supported the school-based EEG planning, artifact awareness, and the need to track data quality carefully in real classroom environments.

- Jiang, X., Bian, G.-B., & Tian, Z. (2019). *Removal of Artifacts from EEG Signals: A Review*. Sensors, 19(5), 987.
  Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6427454/
  Why it matters: this helped justify artifact logging and cautious interpretation of EEG data, especially in movement-heavy or naturalistic child settings.

- Tymofiyeva, O., & Gaschler, R. (2021). *Training-Induced Neural Plasticity in Youth: A Systematic Review of Structural and Functional MRI Studies*. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 14, 497245.
  Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7848153/
  Why it matters: this shaped the wording around neuroplasticity. ChoiceTrain should talk about training-related neural change cautiously and not overclaim that EEG alone proves new neural pathways.

## Feature-to-Paper Mapping

The papers above were used to support these broad feature areas:

- **CPAT task families and family-specific dashboards**
  Supported mainly by Shalev et al. (2007), Muller Spaniol et al. (2021), and Kirk et al. (2017).

- **Teacher/Admin split, fidelity tools, support logging, and clearer classroom workflow**
  Supported mainly by Brady et al. (2019) and Yadav et al. (2020).

- **Learner choice, personalisation, communication support, and pupil-centred content**
  Supported mainly by DeLeon et al. (2001), O'Brien et al. (2024), Wehmeyer (2020), and Lindsay & Varahra (2022).

- **Gentle gamification and touch-first interface decisions**
  Supported mainly by Lumsden et al. (2016), Prins et al. (2011), and Russo-Johnson et al. (2017).

- **EEG research mode, event markers, artifact logging, and cautious neuroplasticity framing**
  Supported mainly by Brooker et al. (2020), Xu et al. (2022), Jiang et al. (2019), and Tymofiyeva & Gaschler (2021).

## Important Note

ChoiceTrain is still described in this repository as a **teaching and research-support tool**. The papers above were used to inform feature design and future study planning. They do not make the current app a diagnostic, therapeutic, or medically validated intervention.
