# Research Evidence and Peer-Reviewed Papers

This file lists the peer-reviewed papers that have been used to justify or shape major ChoiceTrain features.

These papers inform design choices, classroom workflow decisions, ChoiceTrain Attention Training (AT), and future EEG/research planning. The original CPAT terminology is retained where it is part of a paper title or research description. The evidence does **not** mean that ChoiceTrain is a diagnostic, medical, or clinically validated tool.

## 1. Attention Training Structure and CPAT Evidence

- Shalev, L., Tsal, Y., & Mevorach, C. (2007). *Computerized progressive attentional training (CPAT) program: Effective direct intervention for children with ADHD*. Child Neuropsychology, 13, 382-388.
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1080/09297040600770787
  Why it matters: this is the core CPAT paper. It directly informed ChoiceTrain's four attention families: sustained, selective-spatial, orienting, and executive attention.

- Muller Spaniol, M., Mevorach, C., Shalev, L., Teixeira, M. C. T. V., Lowenthal, R., & de Paula, C. S. (2021). *Attention training in children with autism spectrum disorder improves academic performance: A double-blind pilot application of the computerized progressive attentional training program*.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34227246/
  Why it matters: this paper helped justify using CPAT-style attention work in school-aged autistic learners and reinforced the importance of measuring academic transfer rather than assuming it.

- Kirk, H., Gray, K., Ellis, K., Taffe, J., & Cornish, K. (2017). *Impact of Attention Training on Academic Achievement, Executive Functioning, and Behavior: A Randomized Controlled Trial*. American Journal on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities, 122(2), 97-117.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28257246/
  Why it matters: this informed the decision to keep attention training connected to school outcomes, behaviour, and executive-function measures for SEND learners.

- Kirk, H. E., Raber, A., Richmond, S., & Cornish, K. M. (2021). *Examining potential predictors of attention training outcomes in children with intellectual and developmental disorders*. Journal of Intellectual & Developmental Disability, 46(3), 197-203.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39818559/
  Why it matters: this supported the learner-profile idea that baseline attention, adaptive functioning, and individual differences may affect training response.

- Powell, G., Wass, S. V., Erichsen, J. T., & Leekam, S. R. (2016). *First evidence of the feasibility of gaze-contingent attention training for school children with autism*. Autism, 20(8), 927-937.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26862085/
  Why it matters: this was used as a cautionary paper around transfer. It supports attention training as promising, but also shows that non-trained gains should be measured directly rather than assumed.

## 2. Teacher Fidelity, Prompting, and Classroom Delivery

- Brady, L., Padden, C., & McGill, P. (2019). *Improving procedural fidelity of behavioural interventions for people with intellectual and developmental disabilities: A systematic review*. Journal of Applied Research in Intellectual Disabilities, 32(4), 762-778.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30968529/
  Why it matters: this directly informed ChoiceTrain's fidelity checklist, support logging, and the decision to make teacher workflow clearer and more structured.

- Yadav, S., Chakraborty, P., Kaul, A., Pooja, & Gupta, B. (2020). *Ability of children to perform touchscreen gestures and follow prompting techniques when using mobile apps*.
  Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7303424/
  Why it matters: this informed touch-first task design, prompt simplification, and the preference for visual or animated prompting over more complex instruction formats for young learners.

- Faja, S., Clarkson, T., Gilbert, R., Vaidyanathan, A., Greco, G., Rueda, M. R., Combita, L. M., & Driscoll, K. (2022). *A preliminary randomized, controlled trial of executive function training for children with autism spectrum disorder*.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34474598/
  Why it matters: this helped justify structured, visually clear executive-function tasks and the use of explicit rule supports for autistic learners.

## 3. Personalisation, Choice, Communication, and Support

- DeLeon, I. G., Fisher, W. W., Rodriguez-Catter, V., Maglieri, K., Herman, K., & Marhefka, J. M. (2001). *Examination of relative reinforcement effects of stimuli identified through pretreatment and daily brief preference assessments*. Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis, 34(4), 463-473.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11800185/
  Why it matters: this paper supported the idea that preferences change over time, which is why ChoiceTrain includes personalisation, preferred content, and the possibility of short daily preference-led adjustments.

- O'Brien, K., and colleagues. (2024). *Mand Modality Preference Assessments among High- and Low-Tech Options for Individuals with Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities: A Systematic Review*.
  Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10891038/
  Why it matters: this informed the communication-support and AAC-aware thinking behind learner personalisation and future modality options.

- Wehmeyer, M. L. (2020). *Self-determination in adolescents and adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities*. Current Opinion in Psychiatry, 33(2), 81-85.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31833947/
  Why it matters: this supported adding learner choice, agency, and support for self-determination rather than making the tool purely adult-directed.

- Lindsay, S., & Varahra, A. (2022). *A systematic review of self-determination interventions for children and youth with disabilities*.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34056997/
  Why it matters: this reinforced the value of learner choice, scaffolding, and multi-component supports in educational settings.

## 4. Gamification and Touch-First Design

- Lumsden, J., Edwards, E. A., Lawrence, N. S., Coyle, D., & Munafo, M. R. (2016). *Gamification of Cognitive Assessment and Cognitive Training: A Systematic Review of Applications and Efficacy*. JMIR Serious Games, 4(2), e11.
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27421244/
  Why it matters: this paper shaped the current strategy of using light game framing to improve motivation without letting game elements overwhelm the cognitive task.

- Prins, P. J. M., Dovis, S., Ponsioen, A., ten Brink, E., & van der Oord, S. (2011). *Does computerized working memory training with game elements enhance motivation and training efficacy in children with ADHD?*
  Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20649448/
  Why it matters: this helped justify adding engaging variants and reward structures cautiously, while still protecting the core attentional rule and measurement integrity.

- Russo-Johnson, C., Troseth, G. L., Duncan, C., & Mesghina, A. (2017). *All Tapped Out: Touchscreen Interactivity and Young Children's Word Learning*.
  Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5388766/
  Why it matters: this informed the choice to keep learner interactions simple, relevant, and on-task rather than filling the interface with decorative or off-topic interactivity.

## 5. EEG / ERP and Research Support

- Brooker, R. J., Bates, J. E., Buss, K. A., Canen, M. J., Dennis-Tiwary, T. A., Gatzke-Kopp, L. M., Hoyniak, C., Klein, D. N., Kujawa, A., Lahat, A., et al. (2020). *Conducting Event-Related Potential (ERP) Research With Young Children: A Review of Components, Special Considerations, and Recommendations for Research on Cognition and Emotion*.
  Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8136588/
  Why it matters: this directly informed ChoiceTrain's event-marker planning, shorter blocks, practice structure, and the need for child-friendly pacing during EEG-linked work.

- Xu, K., and colleagues. (2022). *EEG Data Quality in Real-World Settings: Examining Neural Correlates of Attention in School-Aged Children*. Mind, Brain, and Education.
  Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10760994/
  Why it matters: this supported the school-based EEG planning, artifact awareness, and the need to track data quality carefully in real classroom environments.

- Jiang, X., Bian, G.-B., & Tian, Z. (2019). *Removal of Artifacts from EEG Signals: A Review*. Sensors, 19(5), 987.
  Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6427454/
  Why it matters: this helped justify artifact logging and cautious interpretation of EEG data, especially in movement-heavy or naturalistic child settings.

- Tymofiyeva, O., & Gaschler, R. (2021). *Training-Induced Neural Plasticity in Youth: A Systematic Review of Structural and Functional MRI Studies*. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 14, 497245.
  Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7848153/
  Why it matters: this shaped the wording around neuroplasticity. ChoiceTrain should talk about training-related neural change cautiously and not overclaim that EEG alone proves new neural pathways.

## 6. Legibility, Sensory Choice, and V1.7 Presentation

- Kuster, S. M., van Weerdenburg, M., Gompel, M., & Bosman, A. M. T. (2018). *Dyslexie font does not benefit reading in children with or without dyslexia*. Annals of Dyslexia, 68(1), 25-42.
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11881-017-0154-6
  Why it matters: children did not read faster or more accurately in Dyslexie than common comparison fonts. This supports ChoiceTrain offering font choice, scale, spacing and learner validation rather than advertising any typeface as a dyslexia treatment.

- Joseph, H., & Powell, D. (2022). *Does a specialist typeface affect how fluently children with and without dyslexia process letters, words, and passages?* Dyslexia, 28(4), 448-470.
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1002/dys.1727
  Why it matters: results were nuanced, with no word or passage reading advantage when spacing was controlled, although distinctive letters may help letter identification. V1.7 therefore treats Atkinson Hyperlegible Next as a configurable high-legibility option, not a guaranteed reading intervention.

- Stewart, C. R., Sanchez, S. S., Grenesko, E. L., Brown, C. M., Chen, C. P., Keehn, B., Velasquez, F., Lincoln, A. J., & Muller, R.-A. (2016). *Sensory Symptoms and Processing of Nonverbal Auditory and Visual Stimuli in Children with Autism Spectrum Disorder*. Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, 46(5), 1590-1601.
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-015-2367-z
  Why it matters: sensory reports, particularly auditory differences, varied in autistic children and experimental findings were not reducible to one simple deficit. This supports adjustable rather than mandatory audio and visual intensity.

- Chevalier, P., Ghiglino, D., Floris, F., Priolo, T., & Wykowska, A. (2022). *Visual and Hearing Sensitivity Affect Robot-Based Training for Children Diagnosed With Autism Spectrum Disorder*. Frontiers in Robotics and AI, 8, 746311.
  Link: https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2021.746311
  Why it matters: sensory sensitivity was relevant to response during technology-based training. It supports recording and adapting visual and sound settings, while not establishing a universal ChoiceTrain sensory profile.

## 7. Longitudinal Personalisation, Retention, and Interpretation

- Corbett, A. T., & Anderson, J. R. (1994). *Knowledge tracing: Modeling the acquisition of procedural knowledge*. User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction, 4, 253-278.
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01099821
  Why it matters: this is a foundational skill-specific learner-model paper. It supports versioning ChoiceTrain skills and updating state from repeated opportunities, while V1.8 initially keeps its estimates descriptive and inspectable rather than claiming a validated knowledge-tracing model.

- Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). *Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis*. Psychological Bulletin, 132(3), 354-380.
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354
  Why it matters: spacing and retention interval jointly affected later recall across the reviewed experiments. This supports recording practice gaps and delayed maintenance checks, not prescribing one universal interval.

- Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). *Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention*. Psychological Science, 17(3), 249-255.
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x
  Why it matters: delayed testing showed retention benefits from retrieval practice in the studied materials. ChoiceTrain uses this cautiously to justify measuring delayed retention rather than treating immediate session accuracy as durable learning.

- Camerota, M., Willoughby, M. T., & Blair, C. B. (2019). *Speed and accuracy on the Hearts and Flowers task interact to predict child outcomes*. Psychological Assessment, 31(8), 995-1005.
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1037/pas0000725
  Why it matters: reaction time and accuracy interacted when predicting child outcomes. This supports joint visualisation and cautions against using faster responses as a stand-alone improvement signal.

- Ganesan, K., Thompson, A., Smid, C. R., et al. (2024). *Cognitive control training with domain-general response inhibition does not change children's brains or behavior*. Nature Neuroscience, 27, 1364-1375.
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-024-01672-w
  Why it matters: the preregistered randomized child trial found trained-function changes but no broad behavioural or neural transfer. It strengthens the case for active controls, delayed follow-up, direct transfer measures, pre-specified outcomes, and cautious claims about neural plasticity.

## 8. Category Learning and Procedural Category Lessons

- Wimmer, M. C., Steininger, T., Schmid, J., & Wittwer, J. (2023). *Category learning in autistic individuals: A meta-analysis*. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review.
  Link: https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-023-02365-4
  Why it matters: the meta-analysis supports category learning as a relevant area to measure while reporting heterogeneous findings and publication-bias concerns. It informs ChoiceTrain's category-task data collection and strengthens the need to avoid claiming that one in-app task proves category mastery or generalisation.

- Ellawadi, A. B., Fein, D., & Naigles, L. R. (2017). *Category structure and processing in 6-year-old children with autism*. Autism Research, 10, 327-336.
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1002/aur.1652
  Why it matters: category-verification performance was examined through accuracy, response time, and response stability. This supports recording multiple signals for ChoiceTrain's category tasks instead of relying on accuracy alone, while not validating ChoiceTrain's task design.

- Snape, S., Krott, A., & McCleery, J. P. (2018). *Do children with Autism Spectrum Disorder benefit from structural alignment when constructing categories?* Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders.
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-018-3551-8
  Why it matters: benefits from multiple examples and structural alignment were not uniform. This supports learner-specific review of exemplar breadth and careful interpretation rather than assuming that varied examples automatically produce generalisation.

ChoiceTrain's category-by-skill state combines these two evidence strands cautiously: Corbett and Anderson motivate an explicit skill-specific state structure, while Wimmer et al., Ellawadi et al., and Snape et al. support recording category performance through repeated, multi-signal observations and avoiding uniform generalisation assumptions. Recommendation contract 1.5.0 uses those task observations only to propose a constraint-valid, one-session category focus for teacher review. Explicitly confirmed teacher ratings and controlled maintenance/generalisation observations are displayed beside the proposal but are not ranking inputs. This category ranking, its thresholds, and the observation-display design are product-design inferences, not methods tested or endorsed by those papers. They have not been validated as knowledge-tracing, mastery, diagnostic, transfer, intervention, or neural-plasticity measures.

## Standards and Design Sources

The following sources guide implementation but are not peer-reviewed evidence that ChoiceTrain improves an educational or clinical outcome:

- [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) informs contrast, visible focus, keyboard access, reflow, reduced-motion considerations, and target size.
- [Atkinson Hyperlegible Next](https://www.brailleinstitute.org/freefont/) was designed by the Braille Institute for character distinction and low-vision legibility. Its inclusion is licensed and local; it is not presented as a SEND-wide or dyslexia-specific treatment.
- Duolingo is a product inspiration only at the level of general visible-progress and immediate-feedback principles. ChoiceTrain uses original visuals, wording and generated sounds.

The V1.7 semantic colour audit is therefore a standards-based accessibility and product-consistency intervention, not a peer-reviewed claim that a particular palette improves cognition, attention, or SEND outcomes. Automated contrast, non-colour state cues, and consistent semantic roles reduce avoidable interface ambiguity; individual theme suitability still requires observation with learners and practitioners.

## Feature-to-Paper Mapping

The papers above were used to support these broad feature areas:

- **Attention Training task families and family-specific dashboards**
  Supported mainly by Shalev et al. (2007), Muller Spaniol et al. (2021), and Kirk et al. (2017).

- **Teacher/Admin split, fidelity tools, support logging, and clearer classroom workflow**
  Supported mainly by Brady et al. (2019) and Yadav et al. (2020).

- **Learner choice, personalisation, communication support, and pupil-centred content**
  Supported mainly by DeLeon et al. (2001), O'Brien et al. (2024), Wehmeyer (2020), and Lindsay & Varahra (2022).

- **Gentle gamification and touch-first interface decisions**
  Supported mainly by Lumsden et al. (2016), Prins et al. (2011), and Russo-Johnson et al. (2017).

- **V1.7 font choice, sensory controls, and cautious accessibility wording**
  Supported mainly by Kuster et al. (2018), Joseph and Powell (2022), Stewart et al. (2016), and Chevalier et al. (2022), with WCAG 2.2 used as an implementation standard.

- **EEG research mode, event markers, artifact logging, and cautious neuroplasticity framing**
  Supported mainly by Brooker et al. (2020), Xu et al. (2022), Jiang et al. (2019), and Tymofiyeva & Gaschler (2021).

- **V1.8 skill graph, longitudinal trends, retention checks, and interpretable recommendations**
  Cepeda et al. (2006) supports recording practice spacing and delayed observations while cautioning against one universal interval. V1.8 therefore makes the planning interval configurable, freezes the selected value for reproducibility, and reports observed comparisons without claiming that the interval is optimal or that retention occurred between sessions.
  Supported mainly by Corbett and Anderson (1994), Cepeda et al. (2006), Roediger and Karpicke (2006), Camerota et al. (2019), and the transfer caution from Ganesan et al. (2024). These papers inform what should be measured; they do not validate ChoiceTrain's future recommendation rules.

- **V1.8 category matching, group completion, category difference, exemplar breadth, and Research profiles**
  Informed by Wimmer et al. (2023), Ellawadi et al. (2017), and Snape et al. (2018). The papers support category processing as a relevant measurement area and justify collecting accuracy, response time, stability, and exemplar exposure. Snape et al. also cautions that benefits from multiple examples are not uniform. These papers do not validate ChoiceTrain as an intervention or establish conceptual mastery, classroom transfer, or neural change.

- **V1.8 teacher-reviewed category focus**
  This is an explicitly labelled design inference from the skill-specific modelling rationale in Corbett and Anderson (1994) and the heterogeneous category-learning evidence in Wimmer et al. (2023), Ellawadi et al. (2017), and Snape et al. (2018). ChoiceTrain therefore shows the underlying observations, checks that the activity is generatable, requires a teacher decision, applies the focus once, and records overrides. No cited paper validates the current ranking rule or establishes that the suggested category is the best teaching decision for an individual pupil.

## Important Note

ChoiceTrain is still described in this repository as a **teaching and research-support tool**. The papers above were used to inform feature design and future study planning. They do not make the current app a diagnostic, therapeutic, or medically validated intervention.
